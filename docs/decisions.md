# Design decisions

| # | Decision | Why | Alternative rejected or deferred |
|---|----------|-----|---------------------------------|
| D1 | Thursday build is a small pilot (10 workers × 10 dates, 44 same-partition shifts); the full 30 × 60 design is one flag away | A small version that runs end to end shows progress and lets us find problems cheaply | Full design only: slower to debug |
| D2 | Report danger-event counts as they are; never lower the danger limit or raise workloads to create events | Tuning the experiment until it "works" is a form of bias | Changing limits after seeing results |
| D3 | PHS is run one minute at a time, carrying body state between minutes | Weather changes during the shift; pythermalcomfort 4.8.0 accepts and returns the state needed | One steady PHS run per shift: ignores changing weather |
| D4 | Logistic regression first; random forest and gradient boosting in Week 8 | Fast, explainable, enough to prove the pipeline | Deep learning: too little data, harder to explain |
| D5 | One-screen Streamlit dashboard | It is the demo for the lab and the symposium | Separate evaluation app: not needed |
| D6 | Messages replayed in Python on MQTT-style topics, no broker | Tests the message format and fault handling with less infrastructure | Mosquitto broker: network behaviour is out of scope |
| D7 | Workers are balanced across acclimatisation × work-rate groups, and every partition gets every group (stratified split) | Unbalanced splits left test sets that could not contain danger events (see iteration log) | Random split; balancing acclimatisation only |
| D8 | Direct sunlight faded in between cos(solar zenith) 0.05 and 0.15 in the WBGT calculation | Hour-averaged radiation divided by a tiny cos(zenith) gave impossible sunrise WBGT values; a hard cut-off caused a 2 °C jump | Using the raw value; a hard cut-off |
| D9 | Air speed for PHS = 10 m wind × 0.75, clipped to 0.2–3.0 m/s | PHS needs air speed at body height and has a valid range of 0–3 m/s | Using 10 m wind directly |
| D10 | (Adopted 8 Nov 2026, PR #4) Latency is measured two ways: processing time per wearable message (message in, alert decision out), and fault-detection delay (minutes from the first faulty heart-rate reading to WBGT fallback, and back to ML after the fault) | Tutor feedback (29 Oct) asked for explicit latency measures. With one-minute messages, a decision must finish well inside 60 s; fallback must start within the configured limits (2 min offline, 5 identical readings) | End-to-end network latency: there is no broker or device (D6), so it would be invented |
| D11 | (Adopted 8 Nov 2026; heuristic review in progress) Usability is measured by alarm burden (warning minutes and false alarm episodes per shift, already in E3) plus a heuristic evaluation of the dashboard against Nielsen's 10 usability heuristics by two group members | Alarm burden is the usability problem that matters most for warning systems (alarm fatigue). A heuristic review needs no outside participants, so the "no human participants" statement in Section III stays true | User study with classmates (e.g. SUS questionnaire): involves human participants and would need ethics clearance |
| D12 | Heart-rate readings outside 30–220 bpm are treated as missing (finding F2), so a faulty sensor triggers the WBGT fallback. Decided by Rayen, 8 Nov 2026 | Values below 30 or above 220 bpm are not plausible for healthy adult workers; the simulated heart rate stays well inside the range, so results are unchanged | 40–200 bpm: could discard real readings from fit or young workers. No check: garbage values would reach the model |
| D13 | (Answer given by Rayen at the Week 8 demo, 12 Nov 2026; recorded here 17 Nov) The warning goes to two people: the worker's own wearable shows STOP and rest, and the supervisor's site board shows which workers are in warning | The professor asked who receives the alarm. A warning the worker cannot see protects no one, and a supervisor needs the overview to plan the work | Supervisor only: the worker depends on being found. Worker only: nobody can plan relief |
| D14 | (Signed off by Rayen, 16 Nov 2026) New research question: is HeatGuard **at least as safe** as the rules already used in the UAE while giving **far fewer warnings**? | Week 8 showed "earlier than WBGT" cannot be shown: the WBGT rule is on most of the shift, so it always gets the full 30-minute lead. Its weakness is precision, not safety. The professor (12 Nov) asked for an artefact that closes the research gap | Keeping "earlier than WBGT": impossible by construction |
| D15 | (16 Nov) Four comparators, all on top of the legal midday break: C1 break only, C2 Heat Index ≥ 40, C3 Thermal Work Limit, C4 WBGT. Each uses the worker's own work rate and acclimatisation and is computed every minute | These are the rules a UAE site can use today. Computing them every minute is the best case for them | WBGT only: not what UAE sites use most |
| D16 | (16 Nov) Safety margin M = 2 percentage points, with a large data set: 200 training workers (6,000 shifts) and 100 new test workers (4,000 shifts) | With few events a small margin cannot be tested. Rayen first chose 5 points at about 100 events, then 2 points once the data set was large | M = 5 points: easier to pass, weaker claim |
| D17 | (16 Nov) The whole UAE working day is simulated, 06:00 to 18:00 with 45 minutes of work and 15 of rest each hour, and the legal break 12:30 to 15:00 from 15 June to 15 September | Week 8 covered mornings only, so it said nothing about the hours and dates the calendar rule leaves open | Mornings only |
| D18 | (16 Nov) Train on summer 2025, test once on summer 2024 with different workers | A new year and new people is the hardest honest test we can run without field data | Random split inside one summer: weather leaks between train and test |
| D19 | (16 Nov) HeatGuard warns everyone when the Heat Index reaches 54 °C, whatever the model says | A hard ceiling for extreme conditions that does not rely on the sensors | Ceiling at 46 °C: it would be on for 22 to 32% of work hours and remove the benefit |
| D20 | (16 Nov) Workers differ from each other (resting core temperature, drinking, effect of dehydration) and slow down when they get hot (self-pacing). The two pacing numbers were tuned on separate calibration workers so that the share of worker-days passing 38.0 / 38.3 / 38.5 °C matches a field study | Without pacing 74 / 53 / 41% of worker-days passed those limits against 44 / 16 / 6% in the field (see iteration log). Real workers self-pace | Keeping the unrealistic data set: it was discarded without being scored |
| D21 | (16 Nov) The plan is written down and committed before the test set is scored, and the test set is scored exactly once. The code refuses to run without the committed plan and refuses a second run | The morning run of 16 Nov was made before the question was settled and could not be used cleanly (see iteration log) | Re-running until the result looks good |
| D22 | (16 Nov, on training workers only) HeatGuard's threshold is the one with the fewest false alarms among those whose miss rate is at most the safest rule's miss rate plus 1 point. Six inputs and a 30-minute warning hold were added | Asking for 99% of training events was impossible for every model, and the rules miss events too. Without the hold the warning flickered (3.5 alarm episodes per shift against 1.6 for the rules) | A fixed 99% target; no hold |
| D23 | (16 Nov, approved with the plan) Against each rule HeatGuard must give at least 50% fewer warning minutes **or** be clearly safer | TWL almost never warns, so "50% fewer warnings than TWL" is impossible although TWL misses most events | Requiring 50% fewer warnings against every rule |
| D24 | (17 Nov) Checks made after the locked run are labelled exploratory. Their plan was committed before any of them ran (`posthoc_plan_v5.md`), every result is reported, and none can change the locked verdict | The marking review found questions a careful reader would ask. Answering them openly is better than leaving them | Changing the locked analysis; not checking at all |
| D25 | (17 Nov, professor's answer passed on by Rayen) Published averages from field studies are the reference for how realistic the simulated workers are | Raw data of real workers could not be found publicly. The professor accepted averages as references, although raw data would be preferred | Claiming the data are real; dropping the comparison |
| D26 | (17 Nov) The demo is one browser page in which the alert engine itself runs (a JavaScript port), checked minute by minute against the Python engine | It needs no installation on the demo machine, it can be shared as a link, and the viewer can break a sensor and watch the response | Extending the Week 8 Streamlit dashboard: needs Python on the demo machine and replays stored results only |
| D27 | (17 Nov) Problems found after the locked run are reported and not fixed in v0.5: the Heat Index comparator does not use Dubai's own chart, and a sensor fault can leave extra warnings for the rest of the day (finding F3) | Changing the model or the rules after seeing the test results would make the locked result meaningless | Quietly fixing and re-running |
